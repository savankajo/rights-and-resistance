The Indian Act

Federal control, First Nations resistance, and the struggle over repeal

Savan Kajo

Federal control Ceremony and belonging White and Red Papers First Nations organizing Sources

How did the Indian Act shape First Nations cultures and identities, and why did some First Nations leaders oppose a federal plan to repeal it? The two questions come together in the debate over Canada’s 1969 White Paper. The proposal reopened questions about who could define legal belonging, govern reserve lands, and decide the terms of reform.

The Act’s effects become clearer when we ask who had the authority to make decisions. Federal officials exercised power over legal belonging, reserve lands, and community governance. When Ottawa proposed repeal, the Indian Chiefs of Alberta argued that reform had to begin with recognition of treaty obligations and First Nations participation in deciding what would replace the Act. Their response shows why resistance to the Act’s controls and defence of specific legal protections could be part of the same political strategy (Indian Chiefs of Alberta, 1970; Manzano-Munguía, 2011).

From colonial laws to federal control

The 1876 Indian Act consolidated earlier colonial laws into a federal statute. Its opening sections placed the supervision of First Nations affairs and the management of reserves, lands, money, and property under federal authority. The Act also defined who counted as “Indian” for legal purposes. These provisions made federal officials responsible for deciding both who fell under the law and how many of the resources governed by it would be administered (Indian Act, 1876, ss. 1–3; Leslie & Maguire, 1984).

Leslie and Maguire’s legislative history traces how these powers developed before and after Confederation. Its focus on statutes and departmental decisions helps explain how the state built the system. Manzano-Munguía connects that legislative history to its purposes. Her study shows how governments could describe policies as protective while using legal classifications, reserves, and residential schooling to pursue assimilation. Reading the two works together helps explain why expanded oversight of First Nations communities could be presented as protection by the same officials who limited their authority (Leslie & Maguire, 1984; Manzano-Munguía, 2011).

The original law’s reserve surrender procedure illustrates how a safeguard could coexist with federal supervision. Section 25 generally barred sale or lease of reserve land until it had been released or surrendered to the Crown. Section 26 required the assent of a majority of eligible adult male band members at a meeting attended by the Superintendent-General or an authorized officer. The proposed surrender then went to the Governor in Council, which could accept or refuse it. These conditions gave the eligible voters a role and restricted direct disposal of reserve lands. The federal government still set the rules for whose agreement counted, supervised the process, and held the final authority over acceptance (Indian Act, 1876, ss. 25–26).

This example also helps distinguish reserves from treaties. The 1876 statute defined a reserve as land set apart for a band’s use or benefit by treaty or otherwise, with legal title vested in the Crown. In that framework, a treaty could be part of the history of a reserve, while the Act supplied federal procedures governing what could happen to it. The sources therefore document two legal relationships that overlapped. This distinction matters to the later debate because the Alberta chiefs based their demands on treaty obligations and still had reason to ask which statutory protections would survive repeal (Indian Act, 1876, s. 3; Indian Chiefs of Alberta, 1970, sections B.5–6).

Ceremony, family, and belonging

The potlatch ban shows what federal control could mean in a particular cultural context. Parliament moved to outlaw potlatching in 1884. For Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw communities, potlatches are occasions to give names, mark life events, and witness the transfer of responsibilities. Authorities prosecuted participants and confiscated ceremonial belongings after Chief Dan Cranmer’s 1921 potlatch. Communities continued to hold potlatches and later sought the return of their belongings. Parliament removed the ban in 1951, and Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw organizers eventually secured the return of many items now held at the U’mista Cultural Centre (UBC Indigenous Foundations, n.d.-b; U’mista Cultural Society, n.d.-b).

U’mista’s account also shows why the losses extended beyond the confiscated items. A potlatch is a setting in which guests witness changes such as naming and the assumption of responsibilities. Interfering with the gathering therefore affected how knowledge and obligations could be shared across families and generations. The effort to bring the collection back to Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw communities shows the difference between lifting a ban and repairing its effects. Lifting the ban made it possible to hold potlatches openly. Recovering confiscated belongings required further work by the people whose families had lost them (U’mista Cultural Society, n.d.-b).

Federal rules also reached into family life. Under former provisions, a woman with status could lose it through marriage to a man without status, while a man with status could confer it on his wife. Parliament amended these rules through Bill C-31 in 1985. In 2019, remaining provisions of Bill S-3 came into force and removed a date restriction that had limited registration for some descendants of women who had lost status through marriage (Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada, 2019; UBC Indigenous Foundations, n.d.-a).

Chelsea Vowel distinguishes federal registration from belonging to a community. Pamela Palmater extends that analysis to the question of who sets the rules for citizenship. Section 6 of the current Act governs registration, while section 10 allows a band to assume control of its membership list if it meets specified conditions. A First Nation may also understand belonging through ties of kinship and community. The distinction matters because a federal administrative decision can affect legal recognition while a Nation determines belonging on its own terms (Indian Act, 1985, ss. 6, 10; Palmater, 2011; Vowel, 2011; Vowel, 2016, chap. 3).

The registration categories show why the consequences can be different for members of the same family across generations. Consider a parent entitled to registration under subsection 6(1) and a second parent with no entitlement. Their child can generally qualify under subsection 6(2). If that child later has a child with someone who also has no entitlement, the next generation generally cannot qualify through that line alone. The rule can produce different outcomes for relatives who share family and community ties. This example helps explain Palmater’s concern about the long-term effects of making a federal classification central to decisions about First Nations citizenship (Indian Act, 1985, s. 6; Palmater, 2011).

The dispute over repeal, 1969–1971

In 1969, Minister of Indian Affairs Jean Chrétien presented the White Paper. The federal government framed equality as participation in Canadian society under common laws and through the same public agencies. It proposed repealing the Indian Act, ending distinct federal legal status, transferring many responsibilities to the provinces, and changing the legal framework for reserve lands. The paper acknowledged disadvantage and presented these changes as a way to free First Nations from discriminatory treatment (Department of Indian Affairs and Northern Development, 1969; UBC Indigenous Foundations, n.d.-c).

The Indian Chiefs of Alberta gave a different account of equality in their 1970 response, Citizens Plus, commonly called the Red Paper. They argued that access to services had to be understood in light of the Crown’s continuing treaty commitments. They defended reserve land held for collective benefit and opposed giving individuals the right to sell it. At the same time, they argued that the Act vested too much authority in the minister and could be revised to give First Nations more control over reserve land and local government. They put the recognition of treaty obligations first and called for review of the Act once those obligations were addressed (Indian Chiefs of Alberta, 1970, sections B.3, B.5–6, and D.1–2).

The proposals for reserve land reveal a specific point of disagreement. The White Paper raised the possibility of full ownership in fee simple and also acknowledged that there were intermediate arrangements and that bands should make their own decisions about how to take control. The Alberta chiefs pointed to a different way to achieve greater control. Their paper proposed keeping Crown title as a safeguard for collective use while changing the Act so that First Nations could exercise more authority over their lands. The two documents shared a stated interest in increasing First Nations control. The chiefs made protection against future sale a condition of that transfer of authority (Department of Indian Affairs and Northern Development, 1969, “Indian Lands”; Indian Chiefs of Alberta, 1970, section B.6).

Education makes the disagreement over services concrete. The White Paper favoured providing services through the same agencies that served other Canadians. The Red Paper argued that education was a treaty obligation and called for funds to be directed to First Nations councils. Under its proposal, communities could operate their own schools or contract with nearby school systems and have a voice in their administration. Access to education mattered to both sides. The chiefs’ response brought two additional questions into the debate: who was accountable for providing education, and who could make decisions about how it was delivered? (Department of Indian Affairs and Northern Development, 1969, “The New Policy”; Indian Chiefs of Alberta, 1970, section D.2).

Harold Cardinal, a Cree leader and president of the Indian Association of Alberta, had made a related argument a year earlier in his own book, The Unjust Society. He called the statute discriminatory, but described it as “a lever in our hands” (Cardinal, 1969/1999, p. 119). The leverage came from insisting that Ottawa deal with unresolved rights before dismantling the existing framework. Where the federal government offered equal treatment through common institutions, Cardinal and the chiefs asked which government would remain answerable for promises the Crown had already made (Cardinal, 1969/1999; Crane Bear, 2015; Indian Chiefs of Alberta, 1970).

How First Nations organized

The Red Paper grew from organizing that predated the White Paper. Leon Crane Bear traces the Indian Association of Alberta’s advocacy on treaties and amendments to the Act back to the 1940s. The Alberta chiefs drew on that experience to put forward their own policy proposals in 1970. The National Indian Brotherhood adopted the Red Paper as its official response, taking the Alberta chiefs’ position into a national debate (Crane Bear, 2015).

In British Columbia, Rose Charlie, Philip Paul, and Don Moses helped bring First Nations representatives together in Kamloops in 1969. The gathering led to the formation of the Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs. Its 1970 response, known as the Brown Paper, made recognition of continuing Aboriginal title a central demand. The Alberta and B.C. responses shared an opposition to Ottawa’s terms of repeal and reflected distinct political priorities. The federal government formally withdrew the White Paper in 1971 (Crane Bear, 2015; UBC Indigenous Foundations, n.d.-c; Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs, n.d.).

The B.C. response, formally titled A Declaration of Indian Rights: The B.C. Indian Position Paper, gives substance to the difference. It treated land as a basis of cultural identity and made the legal status of First Nations lands a question for governments to confront. Where Alberta’s response repeatedly returned to the commitments associated with its treaties, the B.C. position paper addressed the province on the question of title and sought greater First Nations authority in administering land. Reading both responses together shows that national opposition to the White Paper could accommodate distinct regional demands. First Nations organizers developed policy arguments suited to their own political and legal circumstances (Crane Bear, 2015; Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs, 1970).

A later constitutional development helps put the withdrawal of the White Paper in perspective. Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognized and affirmed existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. Its text did not settle how particular treaty commitments should be interpreted, and the Indian Act continued to operate. The two instruments together show why the questions raised in 1969–1971 remained relevant after the immediate policy dispute ended. The recognition of rights, the fulfilment of particular obligations, and reform of federal administration required different kinds of action (Constitution Act, 1982, s. 35; Crane Bear, 2015; Indian Act, 1985).

Conclusion

The Indian Act shaped First Nations cultures and identities by giving federal officials power over legal recognition, reserve life, and cultural practice. The effects are visible in the prosecutions that followed the 1921 potlatch and in registration rules that affected women and their descendants. First Nations people also exercised political and cultural agency throughout this history. Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw communities continued to potlatch and worked to recover confiscated belongings. In Alberta and B.C., First Nations organizers built institutions and put forward their own accounts of what Canada owed and what reform should accomplish.

The repeal debate clarifies the issue that links these histories. Replacing the Act could change a federal statute while leaving unresolved who had the power to decide what came after it. The Red Paper treated Canada’s treaty obligations and First Nations authority as starting points for reform. This is what makes the 1969–1971 debate relevant to understanding the Act’s legacy. The question of self-determination concerns who can define belonging, govern collectively held land, and decide the terms of political change.

Note on terminology. “Indian” appears in statutory language, historical titles, and quotations. This article uses First Nations or specific Nation and community names elsewhere.
